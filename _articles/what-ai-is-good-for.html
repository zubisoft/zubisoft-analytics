Of the 85 working sessions in my logs (those with three or more messages), 26 went right without a single correction. The other 59 needed at least one, and 11 needed five or more. That sounds damning. It isn't, once you look at the other side of the ledger.

Where it earned its keep

Some of the best finds of my year came from the AI. Almost none of them came from the question I had actually asked.

  • A stuck warehouse load. While answering a conversion question, it noticed that "new customers" read zero across the whole book for three days. A load had stalled. We fixed it during the analysis and put the missing rows back.
  • Money nobody was billing. Routine billing checks turned up a large block of transactions that had never been invoiced, plus duplicate billing rows that were then cleaned up.
  • A dead backup route. A failover route was quietly accepting almost nothing: tens of thousands of attempts in one day, a couple of dozen approved.
  • A card-testing attack. A colleague's AI log search spotted it, an engineer validated it, and the merchant's fraud team acted on it.
  • Fact-checking the humans. A human-written draft claimed "over 20%" of failed payments were saved. The AI recounted, the real figure was about half that, and it was fixed before publication.

These have one thing in common. They were found while the agent was doing something else, because it checked a hundred cuts of the data that I would never have had time to check. That is the superpower: breadth, speed, and never getting bored.

The split of work that holds up

The AI brings

  • Breadth: every segment, every day, every provider
  • Speed from question to first draft
  • Tireless reconciliation and re-checking
  • Clear, structured write-ups

The person brings

  • Definitions: what "success" or "new customer" really means here
  • Business context and history
  • A sense of roughly what the answer should be
  • The final call on anything that leaves the building

Operating rules we now use

  1. Treat AI output as directional. Validate any exact number before it goes to a customer, a board or the press.
  2. Ask narrow questions. Narrow questions come back sharp. Broad, many-way segmentations add a lot of noise.
  3. Make it label its claims. One engineer's prompt tags every statement as sourced or inferred, which makes review much faster.
  4. Reconcile every number against a second source before you believe it.
  5. Look at one raw record before you believe a story.
  6. Never let "typically" out of the building without counting all of them.

The 26 clean sessions weren't luck. They were mostly narrow, well-defined questions on data the agent's defaults already covered. The 59 messier ones are where the value was too: the agent did the legwork, and a person made the judgement.

The queries are the easy part now. Asking "does this make sense?" is the job. You are the test suite.
