Every one of these answers was specific, persuasive and one step from being shipped: into an article, a quarterly review or an engineering fix. Every one fell to a short question from someone who knew the domain. None was a SQL error. Each was a judgement call that an experienced analyst makes without thinking.

1. "We typically recovered 80–90%"

The claim. We were drafting an article about what happens when a payment provider goes down. The AI wrote a lovely sentence: when a single provider failed, we "typically recovered 80–90% of affected payments within minutes". It was quotable and shareable.

The reality. It had taken the three best outages and called them typical. Across all of them, most recovered nothing and the total was under 2%. And it wasn't "within minutes": failover happens inside the same payment request, in seconds.

The question that killed it: "Evidence?"

In fairness, in the same stretch of work the AI fact-checked a human-written draft that claimed "over 20%" of failed payments were saved. The real figure was about half that. Humans overclaim too. The AI just does it in flawless prose.

2. 96 payments hit by a bug

The claim. A colleague's AI log-search tool reported that a 3-D Secure bug had hit 96 payments, and an engineering fix was being rushed out.

The reality. My data agent counted again, and one look at the raw API response confirmed it: one payment in seven days.

The question that killed it: "Show me one."

3. A merchant at 0% all weekend

The claim. A major merchant was "completely broken", with nothing getting through and money lost every hour.

The reality. About 91% success, a normal weekend. A success column was wrong and the AI trusted its name. The full story is here.

What caught it: reconciling against a second data source.

4. A provider gap 2.4× too big

The claim. Most of one wallet's payments were failing at provider A. Route them to provider B, it said, and win a large number of approvals every week. It was very persuasive.

The reality. Those payments were already retried on provider B automatically, and fewer than 3% got through on the second try. The agent was reading the final outcome rather than the first attempt. That inflated provider A's gap by 2.4×, and the "fix" was something we were already doing.

The question that killed it: "I can't replicate that. Are you using the initial provider?"

5. A routing choice costing 4.3 points of conversion

The claim. Sending a merchant's traffic to provider B instead of provider A was costing 4.3 percentage points of conversion.

The reality. Provider B was getting more of a card network that approves less often everywhere. Like for like, the two providers were 0.1 points apart, a dead heat. That is mix, not rate. Try the mix-vs-rate demo on our homepage to see how easily it happens.

The question that killed it: "Same card network?"

Bonus: two numbers that can't both be true

The overall acceptance rate for a merchant was 91%. The AI handed me a breakdown in which Visa was below 91% and Mastercard was also below 91%. My reply: "if acceptance is 91%, Visa and Mastercard can't both be below 91%." Its reply: "My mistake." A first-year analyst runs that check in their sleep. The AI skipped it entirely: rows from a different request type had crept into the breakdown.

The five traps underneath

Line up every wrong finding and almost all of them fall into a handful of traps:

  1. The best case becomes "typically". Count the whole population before generalising.
  2. A plausible story replaces a check. Look at one raw record before explaining a mechanism.
  3. The column name is believed. Read how a field is built, not what it is called.
  4. The final outcome stands in for the first attempt. Attribute every decline to the attempt that returned it.
  5. Mix is mistaken for rate. Compare like for like, with volumes, before saying anything got better or worse.
None of these needed a better model. Each needed one question from someone who knew roughly what the answer should be.
